## Additional Guidance on Ad Copy

A common pattern: Parents don't say "my student" ("your student" in ad copy), they say **"my child"**.

### #1. Corporate/Marketing Language (Not Parent Language)

**Examples:** "unlock potential," "maximize score potential," "tailored support," "custom strategies," "growth areas," "concrete score gains," "dream college within reach."

Parents don't talk like this.

**Replace with plain and direct speech:** "raise your child's score"; "tutoring" (instead of "prep" or "support")

### #2. Fake Urgency + Bad Cold-Fit CTAs

**Examples:** "spots filling fast," "limited enrollment," "secure their spot," "don't miss out."

We want to avoid fake scarcity. It's not the real thing that drives action anyway.

**Alternate:** The calendar has built-in urgency: test dates, college application deadlines, weeks remaining, number of sessions possible.

### #3. Lack of Mechanism / Specific Process

**Example:** "The digital SAT demands specific strategies..."; "personalized/expert/data-driven" (telling instead of showing)

**Alt:** Describe specifically how the digital SAT is different: The multi-modal format, where if you score poorly in section 1 it caps your score; or the built-in calculator + tools that you can use to solve questions in 15 seconds instead of 75 with paper + pencil.

### #4. Promise Lacks Specificity

"200 points" is what most of our parents want, so we get this directionally correct, but "gain 200 points" is impossible for anyone over 1400, and if you're above 1350 it's probably stretching believability.

The claim can be made more credible with (almost any) conditions:

- 16 sessions → 200 points
- 8 sessions + 20min/day practice = 100 points per month
- If your SAT score is between 1100–1300, you can gain 200 points in 16 sessions / in 8 weeks

## Creative Brief — Static Ad

**Persona:** The System Optimizer. Corporate father, 48–55. Treats SAT like a business problem. Already knows his kid's target score, the school's median, and the gap. Needs execution not education. Budget is irrelevant. Process rigor is everything.

**Placement:** LinkedIn single-image feed ad, Google Display, retargeting.

**Objective:** Click to book a diagnostic. Not click to learn more. He doesn't need nurturing. He needs a reason to believe the operation is tight.

**Core Insight:** This man buys outcomes with defined inputs. He doesn't want to hear "we help students reach their potential." He wants scope, method, timeline, result. The ad should read like a project summary, not a marketing asset.

### Concept: "The Gap Report"

The ad looks like an internal performance dashboard or a clean consulting deliverable — not a tutoring ad. White or very light background. Clean sans-serif type. No stock photos of smiling teens. No lifestyle imagery. Data only.

#### Visual Layout — Top to Bottom

**Header** (small, understated, top left): Varsity Tutors logo. Small. Not the hero.

**Headline** (large, bold, centered): "Gap: 190 points. Timeline: 10 weeks. Result: Closed."

**Body** (centered beneath headline, clean two-column layout that reads like a spec sheet):

| INPUT | OUTPUT |
|---|---|
| Starting Score: 1,260 | Final Score: 1,470 |
| Target Score: 1,450+ | Points Gained: 210 |
| Diagnostic Gaps Identified: 3 | Time to Result: 10 weeks |
| Sessions Per Week: 2 | Practice Tests Completed: 9 |
| Session Format: 1:1 | Score Trend: Linear improvement weeks 3–10 |

**Bottom strip** (clean, single line): "Start with a free diagnostic. Know the gap before you spend a dollar."

**CTA button:** "Book Diagnostic" — no "Learn More," no "Get Started." He books or he doesn't.

#### Design Direction

No gradients. No rounded corners on everything. No tutoring-ad-blue. The palette is white, black, and one accent — either a dark navy or a muted green that reads "on track" like a dashboard indicator. Typography should feel like a financial report or a pitch deck, not an ed-tech landing page. If it looks like it could be a McKinsey one-pager, you're in the right zone. If it looks like it could be on the side of a school bus, start over.

No photos of people. No illustrations of students. The absence of imagery is the point. This ad respects his time by giving him only what he needs to make a decision: inputs, outputs, and a clear next step.

#### Variant to Test Against

Same layout. Swap the headline to the ROI frame from call data:

> "Princeton Review: $252/hr for group instruction. Varsity Tutors: 1:1 sessions, diagnostic-driven plan, 210-point avg gain. Do the math."

This version directly attacks the competitive comparison he's already running in his head. He's pricing alternatives. Hand him the answer.

## SAT Messaging Guidance — Do's & Don'ts

### Do

- Use the term **"Your child"** — parents think of them as their children not their students
- Use the term **SAT Tutoring**
- Use avg or typical score gains/improvements
- Talk about **100pts/mth** if you attend 2X/week 1:1 sessions, 1 weekly practice test, and 20 mins per day of practice problems
- Mention that every X points on the SAT is worth on avg Y dollars in scholarship/merit aid
- Compare us to competitors:
  - Self study (Khan Academy, SAT prep books, Acely, apps)
  - In Person Learning Centers (Mathnasium, Sylvan, Kumon, etc.)
  - Local Tutors
  - Expensive Group Classes/Courses (Princeton Review, Kaplan)
- Compare our **results** to competitors:
  - 10x the score improvement of self study
  - 2.6X the score improvement of in person learning centers, local tutors and expensive group courses
- Compare our **value/cost** to competitors:
  - For what Princeton Review and Kaplan charge for group courses ($1500–$2000) we charge for 1:1
  - Princeton Review/Kaplan charge $199–$252/hour for 1:1. We charge as low as $349/mth
- Claim that in-person tutoring puts students at a disadvantage, because the SAT is taken on a laptop and needs to be taught and trained on a laptop. Students need 10's of hours of practice in the same digital environment they'll be tested in. Up to 60% of the math section can be solved with built-in formulas and calculator, but students who aren't trained on it try to do all the math by hand and run out of time

### Don't

- Use the term "your student"
- Use the term "SAT Prep"
- Use a score improvement guarantee
- Use vague score claims w/ no specificity
- Claim we can help them actually get a scholarship
- Market as being "online tutoring" — parents will dismiss us before we can even make the case for why we're better

## High Value Personas

### The Athlete-Recruit Gatekeeper

Almost always a **mother** calling on behalf of her **11th grade student athlete**, occasionally a 10th or 12th grader, from a middle to upper-middle-class household, sometimes single-income or military-connected, often in areas with strong athletic programs such as Texas districts like Fort Bend or Lamar CISD or competitive suburban East Coast communities.

The student plays a recruitable sport such as football, soccer, crew, golf, or basketball, and the SAT is framed not as a general college admissions metric but as a **gating factor for scholarships, NCAA or NAIA eligibility, or coach support**.

These families value their child's athletic dream and believe hard work on the field should not be undermined by a test score, and their core fear is that the SAT becomes the reason a scholarship opportunity disappears while the recruiting window is still open. They often describe very specific academic gaps, especially in math, such as missing foundational Algebra 2 concepts or struggling with word problems that slow them down, and they may express feeling overwhelmed by trying to manage grades, scores, and recruiting timelines simultaneously.

They are typically **late funnel and urgency-driven**, having already decided that tutoring is necessary and looking for a provider who can move quickly and fit into an intense practice and competition schedule. They respond best to signals that you understand recruiting timelines, can deliver focused 1:1 instruction targeting precise weaknesses, have worked with recruited athletes before, and can match a tutor and schedule a first session within 48 hours, including late evening availability if needed.

Common questions revolve around whether meaningful score gains are possible within a tight timeframe, how sessions will fit around sports commitments, whether an online provider is legitimate, and occasionally cost, which is best framed in comparison to potential scholarship dollars.

**Conversion likelihood is very high** because the mother is usually empowered to decide on the spot, urgency is clear, and budget resistance is low, but retention depends heavily on the quality of the first session.

---

### The Proactive Suburban Optimizer

Typically an **upper-middle-class suburban mother** calling on behalf of her **11th grader**, sometimes a 10th grader, in a dual-income household in an affluent district like suburban Chicago, New Jersey, Charlotte, Cumberland Valley, or similar college-focused Midwestern towns.

The student usually has a **3.8+ GPA**, is taking AP classes, and is active in extracurriculars, and the family may already have a college advisor or prior experience from an older sibling. These parents value optimization and fairness and want to make sure their child isn't leaving points on the table compared to peers who prepared more thoroughly.

They are not panicked but are motivated by a clear gap: a PSAT or first SAT score in the mid-1200s that doesn't align with the student's classroom performance. Comments like "1260 and she wants a 1450," or "1250 is just not going to cut it," reflect their belief that the student is capable but needs a strategic boost.

They are typically **mid-funnel**, comparison shopping between one or two options, often weighing an in-person provider or an add-on from a current tutor, and convenience can be a deciding factor. They respond best to specificity rather than hype, wanting to hear a clear plan such as a diagnostic test, gap analysis, and a targeted 30–40 hour plan over 8–10 weeks to reach a 1450.

Common objections include wanting to consult their partner, comparing against an in-person option, questioning online effectiveness, or occasionally discussing price, which is best framed as an investment relative to scholarship potential.

**Strong conversion potential** when both parents are engaged, with the main risk being loss to a local competitor or failure to follow up promptly.

---

### The Immigrant Family Navigator

Typically a first- or second-generation immigrant family, often South Asian, Middle Eastern, or Latin American, with a **mother calling about 60%** of the time or a **student calling as a proxy about 40%** of the time. The student is usually in 10th through 12th grade.

Families are often located in suburban areas like Lake Travis or Fort Bend in Texas, Forsyth County in Georgia, Waukegan, Illinois, Norwalk, Connecticut, or similar communities. There are often signals such as accented English, the student translating for a parent, references to educational systems outside the US, and a strong emphasis on education as the primary path to upward mobility.

These families deeply value credentials, expertise, and structured guidance, and they tend to approach decisions collectively, with the parent acting as advocate, scheduler, and quality controller. Their fears center on making the wrong choice in an unfamiliar system, wasting money on ineffective tutoring, or falling behind peers whose families better understand US college admissions. Their hopes are clear and often tied to specific professional outcomes like medicine, engineering, or finance.

They are usually **early to mid-funnel**, either navigating test prep for the first time or returning after a disappointing tutoring experience, and they require more education about the SAT itself before they can evaluate providers. They respond best to credibility first, including tutor qualifications and success stories, followed by a clear, step-by-step explanation of the process.

**Conversion rates are high** when the financial decision-maker is directly engaged and much lower when a student is calling alone.

---

### The Education-First Cultural Investor

Typically a dual-income professional household with **notably higher father involvement** than most other personas, often with one or both parents holding STEM or technical backgrounds. The student is usually in 10th or 11th grade, enrolled in an accelerated track or STEM academy.

Education spending is a non-negotiable line item in the household budget, and these families have often already invested in multiple resources such as prep books, online programs, YouTube tutorials, and subject-specific tutors. They value **mastery over quick performance gains** and believe the right system and expertise can solve almost any academic issue.

Their trigger is typically a **grade-related concern** rather than a test score, such as a drop in AP Physics or AP Calculus, a teacher moving too quickly, or frustration that prior tutoring did not improve performance. They are **mid-funnel, highly information-driven, and consensus-oriented**, often requiring spousal consultation and sometimes a trial before committing.

Conversion often requires multiple touchpoints, but once committed, these families tend to be loyal, expand into additional subjects, and refer peers, making them **high lifetime value customers**.

---

### The System Optimizer

Typically a dual-income professional household with a **father calling more than 75%** of the time, often in tech-centric regions like the Atlanta metro area, Austin, or the Bay Area, and frequently leveraging employer-sponsored benefits such as Bright Horizons or corporate education stipends.

The parent approaches tutoring as a **procurement decision** rather than an emotional one. These families value efficiency, process optimization, measurable outcomes, and self-service tools, and they are motivated by practical triggers such as an approaching SAT date, an expiring employer benefit, or frustration with a previously cumbersome setup process.

Their concerns are rarely expressed emotionally; instead, they focus on **logistics**: how quickly tutoring can begin, how scheduling and rescheduling work, how progress is measured, and how benefits are applied. They are **late funnel and execution-focused**.

**Conversion likelihood is high** when the process is smooth, but this persona will quickly disengage if enrollment feels clunky, scheduling is slow, or instructions are unclear.

---

### The Neurodivergent Advocate

Almost always a **mother** calling about a 9th through 12th grade student with a diagnosed or suspected learning difference such as ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, dyslexia, dyscalculia, or a processing disorder. She typically has deep knowledge of her child's diagnosis, accommodations, and learning profile.

The parent often provides extensive context about the child's history before asking about tutoring, mentioning extended time, separate testing rooms, therapeutic activities, anxiety around testing, or prior tutoring experiences that failed because the instructor could not adapt.

She values the **right fit over the lowest price** and wants her child to be seen as capable rather than broken. The trigger is often an approaching test date combined with the realization that standard prep will not work.

She is **mid-funnel** and evaluating whether the provider can truly accommodate her child's needs, not whether tutoring in general is worthwhile. She needs clear acknowledgment of the specific diagnosis, honesty about tutor experience with similar students, a detailed explanation of the matching process, and reassurance that switching tutors is easy if the fit is not right.

**Conversion likelihood is high** when the representative listens fully, responds with warmth and specificity, avoids generic promises, and follows up in writing with tutor profiles, an accommodation plan, and session structure.

---

### The Burned Returner

Most often a **mother** calling about an 11th or 12th grade student after a **prior negative tutoring experience**, either with VT or another provider. The defining characteristic is not geography or income level but **disappointment**.

She may reference a specific tutor by name, describe grades declining during tutoring, or use emotionally charged language like "burned," "wasted," or "disappointed," and she often sets explicit conditions for returning, such as requesting a particular tutor or format.

This parent values accountability, professionalism, and real results. Her core fear is being burned again. She is typically **late funnel but conditional**, not browsing but returning out of urgency due to a new academic crisis.

What she needs first is **acknowledgment and ownership** of the past failure, without excuses, followed by a concrete explanation of what will be different this time.

**Conversion likelihood is moderate** and highly dependent on whether trust can be rebuilt in a single conversation.
